The problem

We are swapping the sea for concrete

Projects like Diyar Al Muharraq create new land by filling in the sea. Water stays cool in the summer sun. The sand, concrete and asphalt that replace it heat up much more, and the neighbourhoods built on top live with that heat.

Hotter neighbourhoods

Surface heat builds up on the new land, where thousands of people will live, walk and work.

Measured too late

Heat is rarely checked after a project is built, so planners don't know which streets need shade first.

Generic guidelines

Global design rules don't capture Bahrain's specific coast, climate and building materials.

Our solution

Sidra: satellite evidence for coastal heat planning

Sidra turns free, open satellite archives into three things planners can act on.

1. Measure

Compare reclaimed and existing land on the same summer dates, matching measured surfaces and distance from water.

2. Locate

Find the hotspots that stay hot summer after summer, and check whether nearby existing land is warming too.

3. Act

Shortlist the cells a field team should inspect first, so shade, greenery or material changes go where they matter.

How it works

From satellite to decision

Everything runs on open data and open-source tools, so the analysis can be repeated for any coastline.

  1. Find the new landTrack the water index (MNDWI) year by year with Landsat to see where sea turned into land, and when.
  2. Measure the heatMatch inland reclaimed and existing-land cells using surface cover, vegetation, development and coastline distance; compare same-date temperatures.
  3. Map persistent hotspotsRun a Getis–Ord Gi* hotspot test on 90 m cells and keep only the places that stay in the hottest 10% on most dates.
  4. Check the surroundingsCompare nearby existing land with control land far away, and test how sure we are by resampling whole years and areas.
  5. Model and shortlistTrain a gradient-boosting model on satellite indicators and ERA5 weather, then flag cells that are repeatedly hotter than predicted.
Why satellites?
  • Optical history identifies reclamation; recent thermal scenes compare similar land on the same dates.
  • Cover entire islands at once instead of a few weather stations.
  • Free and open: Landsat (USGS), Sentinel-2 (ESA) and ERA5 (Copernicus).
  • Repeatable for any new project in Bahrain or the Gulf.

Honest limits

What the satellites can and can't tell us

Surface, not air

Landsat measures late-morning ground temperature, not the air people breathe or how hot it feels on the street.

Evidence, not proof

The comparisons show strong patterns, but they can't on their own prove that reclamation caused every degree.

Ground truth needed

Ground sensors, material checks and monitored trials are needed before choosing the right cooling measure.

Why "Sidra"?

Named after a tree that survives the heat

The sidr tree (Ziziphus spina-christi) is one of Bahrain's most loved native trees. It thrives in extreme heat and little water, and has given shade for generations. We want Bahrain's new islands to be just as resilient.
